Research

Generally speaking, I really enjoy areas where combinatorics, optimization, and analysis meet. I am particularly drawn to the foundational side of problems: finding the right concepts for the model at hand and then rigorously proving existence and structural properties of solutions, while paying careful attention to the details.

Dynamic flows and tolls

Dynamic flows describe how traffic, data or goods move through a network over time, with congestion that builds up and dissolves. My doctoral thesis proves a decomposition theorem for dynamic flows, characterizes the flows that tolls can implement as equilibria, and shows that system optimal flows, unlike static ones, are not always implementable. In contrast, for well-behaved single-destination networks, the implementability of system optimal flows is proven.

Generalized Nash equilibrium problems

Generalized Nash equilibrium problems are games in which the strategies a player may choose depend on the choices of the others. I study them with mixed-integer variables, where the players' problems are nonconvex. So far, I have studied them along three questions: how their equilibria can be characterized by convexification, when these equilibria are rational (a prerequisite for exact algorithms), and how branch-and-cut methods compute an equilibrium, exact or approximate, or prove that none exists.

Pricing in nonconvex games and markets

Tolls for dynamic flows are one instance of a more general question: which outcomes can prices on resources enforce as an equilibrium? Players with nonconvex, for example mixed-integer, strategy spaces pay for every unit of a resource they consume, and the question is for which targeted aggregate consumption there are prices that induce an equilibrium with exactly this consumption. One duality-based framework covers this question for general finite-dimensional resource allocation games. If such a game does not admit suitable prices, we study market-clearing prices with minimal regret.

About the network beside the text. On a phone it is a single edge from s to t with a steady stream of flow; on a wider screen it looks as follows. Two commodities (green and blue) enter at the source s and aim to traverse the network to reach the common destination t. The flow propagation model is the Vickrey point-queue model. Each edge e is a pipe with free-flow transit time τe corresponding to the length of the pipe. Moreover, the width of the pipe corresponds to its capacity νe. When more flow arrives at the end of an edge than it can pass, its walls turn orange and a point queue forms there, drawn as a container: its fill level is the queued volume ze, its colour the waiting time ze/νe. Flow that enters edge e at time θ reaches its end at time θ + τe, so what arrives at the queue at time θ is the inflow rate f+e(θ − τe) of τe time units earlier. As long as the queue is not empty, it lets flow leave at rate νe, so its volume changes by arrivals minus capacity, and flow that arrives at time θ waits ze(θ)/νe. An empty queue starts to grow only when more than νe arrives:

z′e(θ) = f+e(θ − τe) − νe  while  ze(θ) > 0.

Flow leaving a node enters the edges on currently shortest routes, counting the waiting times of the queues it sees at that moment (τe + ze/νe): a time-discretised instantaneous dynamic equilibrium (cf. e.g. the thesis of Graf). The two commodities differ only in how they weigh tolls. Starting with the first paper on tolls in the publication list, one edge in each stage charges a toll pe (magenta), and commodity k counts it like βk units of time: the experienced edge cost is then τe + ze/νe + βkpe, with βk = 0.5 for blue and 2 for green. The toll sits where the best other route branches off and equals its extra time, so blue pays and stays on the fastest route while green switches. Without tolls both would share the fastest routes; with them the stream splits, and the capacities leave room for both routes, so no queue forms. Time θ is tied to your scroll position. Note that in my thesis, I consider a different equilibrium concept, namely full information equilibria in which particles choose at the start one walk from the source to the destination with the full knowledge of the other flow particles. The instantaneous equilibrium concept was used here as these equilibria are easier to compute.

The sink t is the root of a branch-and-cut search for a pure Nash equilibrium of a small integer game, following our branch-and-cut method for mixed-integer Nash equilibrium problems. Two firms choose integer quantities x1, x2 (Cournot competition). Open nodes are processed breadth-first, first in, first out: level by level, in the order in which they were created. Each node minimises Σi πi(x) − ηi over a relaxation, where ηi is a relaxed proxy variable for the best-response value Φi(x−i). A node with positive value contains no equilibrium and is pruned (red cross); a fractional solution is branched on; if x* is integral but no equilibrium, best-response cuts ηi ≤ πi(yi*, x−i) with best replies yi* to x*−i are added, valid in the whole tree (blue bars). A cut removes no strategy profile: it acts in the space of (x, η), where it lowers the ceiling on ηi to the cost πi(yi*, x−i) of playing yi*. With every cut, ηi approximates the best-response value Φi(x−i) more closely from above; the point (x*, η*) is cut off, while x* itself stays feasible. Solved again, the node keeps its region: only its solution x* and its value change, and the value can only rise. The search stops at the first x* with Ψ(x*, y*) = 0 (blue disc). The tree is a real run of the method.

On wide screens the left margin follows the search in the space of strategy profiles: the best responses BR1 and BR2, whose intersection is the equilibrium, pruned regions in red, the box of the current node, its solution x*, arrows for profitable deviations, and the node's value against the pruning threshold 0. After a cut the box stays as it is, since nothing is removed in this space; only x* and the value move.
